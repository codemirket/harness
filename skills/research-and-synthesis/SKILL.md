# Research and synthesis

Frame the decision, audience, time horizon, geography, and evidence needed.
Separate discovery, verification of a claim, comparison of options, and systematic
review: each needs a different search depth. Reuse existing evidence when current.

## Build the evidence set

- Split the question into claims that can be checked independently. Identify
  which claims need primary data, official documentation, expert analysis, or
  lived experience. Use sources suited to the claim rather than a fixed hierarchy.
- Search with alternate terminology, competing explanations, and disconfirming
  queries. Follow citations to original material and record search terms when
  reproducibility matters. Treat search snippets as leads, not read sources.
- Open relevant sources. Capture author/publisher, publication and event dates,
  scope, methods, limitations, exact URL or stable identifier, and the supporting
  passage or data location. Keep quotations distinct from paraphrase.
- For technical questions, check official documentation against the actual version.
  For research papers, distinguish peer-reviewed findings, preprints, replications,
  and commentary. Check retractions/corrections where they affect conclusions.
- For web extraction, record retrieval time and pagination/coverage. Respect access
  controls; authenticated browser access does not authorize bulk redistribution.
- If delegating research, give workers bounded questions and a shared evidence schema.
  Require source links and contradictory findings, not just polished summaries.

## Evaluate and synthesize

Maintain a compact claim ledger: claim, source, evidence location, applicability,
confidence, counterevidence, and unresolved gap. Multiple articles repeating one
press release are one evidentiary origin, not independent confirmation.

Compare sources on definitions, population, dates, denominators, incentives, and
measurement methods before explaining a disagreement. Distinguish absence of
public evidence from evidence of absence. Do not convert a vendor claim, benchmark,
or anecdote into a measured result for the user's setting.

Use calculations only with traceable inputs and units; distinguish estimated
scenarios from observations. Preserve competing explanations when the evidence
cannot resolve them. State what would change the recommendation.

## Deliver a useful answer

Lead with the supported answer or decision. Cite the source beside each material
claim and link to the exact document/page, not search results. Explain important
uncertainty, coverage limits, and dates. Include the evidence ledger or reproducible
method only when it helps the reader audit or reuse the work.

Stop when the decision's important claims have adequate support or the remaining
gap requires new data/access. Report that gap rather than repeating searches.
