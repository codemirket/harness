# Worked case: a paid pilot does not settle viability

Use this fictional case when a founder wants to infer full-price demand or unit
economics from a discounted pilot. Amounts are net of taxes and refunds.

Ten customers each paid $50 for one month. Eight used the service weekly, and two
renewed for the next month at the proposed $100 monthly price. Acquisition cost
was $90 for each pilot customer. Variable service cost is $70 per active customer
per month, including the founder's delivery time at the stated replacement cost.
There are no other costs in this deliberately limited example.

The observations support ten discounted purchases, eight weekly users during
the pilot and two full-price renewals for one subsequent month. They do not
establish ten retained full-price customers, representative market demand or a
stable long-term retention curve. The two renewals may still be useful evidence.

At pilot price, contribution is $50 - $70 = -$20 per customer before acquisition.
At full price, monthly contribution is $100 - $70 = $30. The simplified $90/$30
payback calculation gives three full-price months only if the pilot loss is
ignored. Including that $20 loss requires recovery of $110, so a continuously
retained customer needs four complete full-price billing months to exceed that
amount, assuming timely collections and unchanged costs. The observed cohort
does not yet demonstrate that retention. Shared fixed costs remain unmodeled.

If available cash allows only two further months of service, a paper four-month
payback is not sufficient liquidity evidence. Build the cohort cash forecast,
including churn and costs of serving customers who do not renew.

A useful next test might examine qualified buyers' full-price renewals or reduce
the costly delivery step. Define the target and decision rule from the owner's
constraints before testing. Do not purchase traffic or contact prospects merely
because an experiment was proposed.
