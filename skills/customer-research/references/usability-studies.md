# Task-based usability studies

Use this method when the decision depends on whether intended users can understand
and complete a task in a prototype or product. Distinguish a study plan, analysis
of supplied sessions and authorized participant sessions. A model-generated persona
or expert walkthrough can generate hypotheses; it is not participant evidence.

## Design a task that can reveal the problem

Identify the decision, build, participant context and critical uncertainty. Recruit
or propose actual or likely users relevant to that uncertainty; include excluded
or unsuccessful users when a success-only sample would hide the problem. Outreach,
recording and use of personal data need their own authorization and consent.
Choose realistic data and prevent unintended purchases, account changes or exposure
of private information in the study environment.

Give participants a believable goal without teaching the interface: “Find a plan
that lets this team invite three contractors” is more informative than “Open
Settings and click Guest access.” Define observable success, partial completion,
failure and moderator assistance before examining results. These task and moderation
principles are described in the
[GOV.UK moderated usability guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing).

Use a short pilot to find broken fixtures and leading instructions. Keep task
conditions comparable when comparing versions; account for order, prior exposure
and learning. When assistive technology is relevant, record the participant's actual
setup and avoid treating an unfamiliar substitute as equivalent.

## Preserve what happened

Record the attempted goal, build and state, observed action, outcome and assistance,
with a session/time reference. Keep quotations, behavior and interpretation separate.
Ask neutral follow-ups about an observed difficulty; do not infer the reason for a
pause or failure solely from a facial expression or time spent.

Let the participant attempt recovery before helping where practical, and record
the intervention when help is needed. A participant who completes after being told
where to click has demonstrated assisted completion. Their praise does not convert
it into unassisted success. Distinguish product defects, comprehension barriers,
test-fixture failures and personal preferences before proposing a change.

For comparison, define the success denominator and exclusion rules consistently.
Repeated tasks from one person are not independent participants. A small convenience
sample can reveal a barrier without estimating its population prevalence or proving
conversion lift. Use `experiment-design` when the decision requires a causal or
quantitative comparison; do not invent confidence from a fixed participant count.

## Deliver a decision with its limits

A plan should contain the participant rationale, neutral tasks, observation rules,
needed environment and data handling. A findings report should connect each claim
to observed sessions, counterexamples, affected tasks and practical impact. Rank
barriers by the consequence and recoverability actually observed; keep proposed
solutions as hypotheses. Redact participant identifiers in shared artifacts.

As a failure probe, revisit a claimed success and inspect whether it depended on
moderator help, a prepared account, prior task learning or an unavailable prototype
path. Report those limits and the next useful observation. A polished screenshot,
five positive interviews or completed task script does not establish usability.
